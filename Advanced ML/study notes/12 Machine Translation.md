## 12. Machine Translation

## Study Notes

### 1. 🌍 Introduction to Machine Translation

Machine Translation (MT) is the process where a computer automatically converts text written in one language (called the source language) into another language (called the target language), while trying to preserve the original meaning. This is a challenging task because languages are very different not just in words but in structure, grammar, and cultural context. The goal of MT is to produce translations that are both accurate in meaning and fluent in the target language.


### 2. 🤔 Why is Translation Hard?

Translation is not simply replacing each word in the source language with a corresponding word in the target language. There are several reasons why this is difficult:

- **Structural Differences:** Languages have different word orders. For example, English typically follows a Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) order ("I eat apples"), while languages like Sinhala, Tamil, and Japanese often use Subject-Object-Verb (SOV) order ("I apples eat"). This means the translation system must reorder words and phrases correctly, sometimes over long distances in the sentence.

- **Morphological Complexity:** Some languages have rich morphology, meaning a single word can carry a lot of grammatical information like tense, number, case, or honorifics. For example, one English word might correspond to a complex inflected form in another language.

- **Lexical Ambiguity and Gaps:** Words can have multiple meanings (e.g., "bank" can mean a financial institution or the side of a river), and sometimes a word in one language has no direct equivalent in another.

- **Missing Information:** Some languages omit information that others require. For example, pro-drop languages like Sinhala often leave out the subject pronoun ("wrote the letter"), so the translation system must infer who performed the action from context.


### 3. ⚙️ Rule-Based Machine Translation (RBMT)

Early MT systems were rule-based, relying on hand-crafted linguistic rules and dictionaries. These systems worked in a pipeline:

- **Analysis:** The source sentence is analyzed using tools like morphological analyzers (which break words into roots and affixes), part-of-speech (POS) taggers, and parsers to understand sentence structure.

- **Transfer:** Using bilingual dictionaries and structural transfer rules, the system maps source language elements to target language equivalents and rearranges them according to target language grammar.

- **Generation:** The target sentence is generated by applying morphological inflections and syntactic rules to produce fluent output.

**Limitations:** Writing comprehensive rules to cover all language complexities is extremely tedious and often incomplete, limiting the quality and scalability of RBMT.


### 4. 📊 Data-Driven Machine Translation

With the availability of large parallel corpora (texts aligned sentence-by-sentence in two languages), data-driven approaches became dominant.

#### Statistical Machine Translation (SMT)

SMT treats translation as a probabilistic problem: given a source sentence, find the target sentence that is most likely to be the correct translation.

- **Noisy Channel Model:** Instead of directly estimating the probability of a target sentence given a source sentence (which is hard), SMT uses Bayes’ theorem to break it down into two parts:
  - The probability of the source sentence given the target sentence (translation model).
  - The probability of the target sentence itself (language model).

- **Phrase-Based SMT:** The system breaks sentences into phrases (not necessarily linguistic phrases, just sequences of words) and estimates translation probabilities for these phrase pairs from data. It also includes a distortion model to penalize unlikely reorderings.

- **Language Model:** This estimates how fluent the target sentence is, usually using n-gram models (probabilities of sequences of words). Smoothing techniques like Kneser-Ney are used to handle unseen word sequences.

- **Decoding:** The system searches for the best target sentence by combining translation and language model scores. Because the search space is huge, pruning techniques are used to keep the search manageable.

**Limitations of SMT:**

- Only local reordering is handled well; long-distance reorderings (like SOV to SVO) are penalized.
- No generalization across word forms (e.g., "walk," "walks," "walked" are treated separately).
- Sparse data problems: unseen phrases get zero probability.
- The pipeline approach means errors accumulate, and components are trained separately, not end-to-end.


### 5. 🤖 Neural Machine Translation (NMT)

NMT revolutionized MT by using deep learning models trained end-to-end, learning to translate directly from data without hand-crafted rules.

#### Encoder-Decoder Architecture

- **Encoder:** A recurrent neural network (RNN) reads the source sentence word by word and compresses it into a fixed-length vector called the context vector.

- **Decoder:** Another RNN generates the target sentence word by word, conditioned on the context vector and previously generated words.

- **Word Embeddings:** Words are represented as dense vectors capturing semantic similarity, allowing the model to generalize better across related words.

#### The Bottleneck Problem

Compressing the entire source sentence into a single fixed-length vector limits performance, especially for long sentences. Important information from early words can be lost, and the decoder cannot revisit the source once decoding starts.

#### Attention Mechanism

To fix this, attention allows the decoder to look back at all encoder states and decide which parts of the source sentence to focus on at each step. This greatly improves translation quality, especially for longer sentences.


### 6. ⚡ Transformer Architecture

Transformers, introduced by Vaswani et al. (2017), removed recurrence entirely, allowing parallel computation and direct connections between all words in a sentence.

- **Self-Attention:** Each word attends to every other word in the same sentence, capturing relationships regardless of distance.

- **Cross-Attention:** In the decoder, attention is applied over the encoder’s output to incorporate source sentence information.

- **Masked Self-Attention:** Prevents the decoder from seeing future words during generation, ensuring proper autoregressive behavior.

- **Multi-Head Attention:** Multiple attention mechanisms run in parallel, each capturing different types of relationships.

- **Positional Encoding:** Since self-attention is order-agnostic, positional information is added to embeddings to preserve word order.

Transformers are now the foundation of nearly all modern MT systems and large language models.


### 7. 🧩 Tokenization and Decoding

#### Subword Tokenization (Byte-Pair Encoding - BPE)

- Full word vocabularies are large and expensive.
- Fixed vocabularies cause out-of-vocabulary (OOV) problems.
- BPE starts with characters and merges frequent pairs to form subword units.
- Frequent words remain whole tokens; rare words break into meaningful subwords.
- This approach handles morphology and OOV words gracefully.

#### Decoding Strategies

- **Greedy Decoding:** At each step, pick the most probable next token. Fast but can get stuck in mistakes.

- **Beam Search:** Keeps multiple hypotheses (beam width k) at each step, scoring them by accumulated probability. Scores are length-normalized to avoid bias toward shorter sentences.

- Larger beams don’t always improve quality and can sometimes worsen it.


### 8. 🌐 Multilingual and Pre-trained Models

#### Multilingual Pre-trained Models

- **mBART:** Pre-trained on many languages with a denoising objective, uses a shared vocabulary and language tokens to specify target language.

- **mT5:** A multilingual version of T5, trained on 101 languages, framing all tasks as text-to-text.

- **Benefits:** Related languages share representations, so low-resource languages benefit from high-resource relatives. Fine-tuning on small datasets can yield usable systems.

- **Trade-off:** Adding many languages can reduce accuracy per language due to fixed model capacity (curse of multilinguality).

#### Massively Multilingual Translation

- **M2M-100:** Translates directly between 100 languages without pivoting through English.

- **NLLB-200:** Extends to 200 languages, focusing on low-resource languages, with an evaluation benchmark (flores-200).

- **Why avoid pivoting?** Pivoting through English can cause loss of information and compound errors.


### 9. 🧠 Large Language Models (LLMs) in Translation

#### Zero-Shot and Few-Shot Translation

- **Zero-Shot:** The model translates based on an instruction prompt without seeing examples.

- **Few-Shot:** The prompt includes a few example translations to guide the model.

- Examples help clarify the task, style, and terminology.

#### Advantages of LLMs

- Can use document-level context for better consistency in pronouns, tense, and terminology.

- Controllable output style (formality, dialect) via prompts.

- Can perform related tasks like post-editing, quality estimation, and explanation.

- No parallel data needed to deploy, useful for very low-resource languages.

#### Limitations of LLMs

- Performance depends on pre-training data; low-resource languages still struggle.

- Off-target translation (wrong language output) is common in multilingual and zero-shot settings.

- Hallucination: fluent but incorrect translations that diverge from the source.

- Higher cost, latency, and less reproducibility compared to smaller dedicated models.


### 10. ✅ Evaluating Translation Quality

#### What Makes a Good Translation?

- There is no single correct translation; multiple valid translations exist.

- Evaluation considers two main human-judged dimensions:
  - **Adequacy:** How well the meaning of the source is preserved.
  - **Fluency:** How natural and well-formed the output is in the target language.

- Human evaluation is the gold standard but is expensive and variable.

#### Automatic Metrics

- **BLEU Score:** Measures n-gram overlap between candidate and reference translations, with clipping to avoid inflated scores from repeated words.

- **Limitations of BLEU:**
  - Only surface-level matching; synonyms or reordering are penalized.
  - Poor correlation with human judgment at sentence level.
  - Less reliable for morphologically rich languages.

- **chrF:** Uses character n-grams, better for morphologically rich languages like Sinhala and Tamil.

- **Neural Metrics (COMET, BLEURT):** Use trained models to score translations, correlating better with human judgments but may be less reliable for low-resource languages due to limited training data.


### Summary

Machine Translation has evolved from rule-based systems to statistical models and now to powerful neural architectures like Transformers. Each step has addressed previous limitations, improving fluency and accuracy. Modern MT leverages large multilingual pre-trained models and LLMs, enabling translation across hundreds of languages, including low-resource ones. However, challenges remain, especially in handling complex linguistic phenomena, low-resource languages, and evaluation. Understanding these concepts is key to appreciating how MT works and where it is headed.
