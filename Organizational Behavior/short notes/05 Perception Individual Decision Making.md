# Organizational Behaviour - Lesson 5: Perception and Individual Decision Making

## Perception
- **Perception** is the process by which individuals organize and interpret sensory impressions to give meaning to their environment.
- What people perceive can differ substantially from objective reality, so people in similar jobs and conditions may form very different impressions of their workplace.
- Perception matters in organizational behaviour because **behaviour is based on perceived reality**, not necessarily on reality itself.

## Factors Influencing Perception
- Perception is shaped by three main sources:
  - **The perceiver** - attitudes, personality, motives, interests, past experiences, and expectations.
  - **The target** - characteristics such as size, intensity, novelty, movement, proximity, and background.
  - **The situation** - time, work setting, social setting, location, light, heat, and other contextual factors.
- People tend to notice distinctive targets and may perceive people they see as similar to themselves more positively than people they see as different.

## Perception Process
- The basic process is **stimuli -> selection -> organization and interpretation -> action**.
- **Receiving stimuli** - information reaches the individual through the senses from external or internal sources.
- **Selection of stimuli** - some information is selected while other information is screened out.
  - External influences include intensity, size, movement, repetition, novelty, familiarity, and contrast.
  - Internal influences include age, learning, interests, self-concept, beliefs, expectations, needs, and cognitive predispositions.
- **Organization of stimuli** turns selected information into a meaningful whole.
  - **Grouping** combines stimuli based on similarity or proximity.
  - **Closure** fills gaps in incomplete information using experience, data, or assumptions.
  - **Simplification** identifies the main features of a situation so it can be interpreted more easily.
- **Perceptual defence** screens or reshapes information that creates conflict or threat, such as denying it, distorting it to match existing beliefs, or treating it as an exception.

## Social Perception and Attribution
- **Social perception** is the process of gathering and interpreting information about other people using cues such as appearance, verbal communication, and nonverbal signals.
- **Attribution theory** explains how people judge whether behaviour is caused internally by the person or externally by the situation.
- Three factors shape attribution:
  - **Distinctiveness** - whether the person behaves differently in different situations. High distinctiveness supports an external attribution.
  - **Consensus** - whether other people behave similarly in the same situation. High consensus supports an external attribution.
  - **Consistency** - whether the person behaves the same way over time. High consistency supports an internal attribution.

## Shortcuts in Judging Others
- **Selective perception** means attending to stimuli according to their relevance, importance, and personal interests.
- **Halo effect** occurs when an overall impression of someone influences judgments about that person's other characteristics.
- **Contrast effect** occurs when a judgment is changed by comparison with a contrasting person, object, or reference point.
- **Social identity effect** occurs when identification with social groups shapes thoughts, emotions, and attitudes toward in-group and out-group members.
- **Stereotyping** means applying generalized beliefs about a category of people to individual members while overlooking individual differences.

## Individual Decision Making
- **Decision-making** occurs as a reaction to a problem.
- A **problem** is a discrepancy between the current state and a desired state that requires consideration of alternative courses of action.
- Perception and decision-making are linked because recognizing that a problem exists and deciding whether action is required are themselves **perceptual judgments**.
- The slides distinguish levels of organizational decisions:
  - **Strategic decisions** are associated with senior management and tend to be broad and relatively unstructured.
  - **Tactical decisions** are associated with middle management and are more focused and semi-structured.
  - **Operational decisions** and routine data processing are more structured and detailed.

## Decision-Making Approaches
### Rational Decision-Making
- The **rational decision-making model** assumes complete information, unbiased identification of all relevant alternatives, and selection of the option with the highest utility.
- The model proceeds by identifying the problem, clarifying decision criteria, identifying alternatives, calculating risks and benefits, evaluating alternatives, and selecting the best option.
- In practice, many significant decisions rely on judgment rather than a complete rational process.

### Bounded Rationality
- **Bounded rationality** recognizes limits in knowledge, cognitive capacity, time, and other resources.
- People therefore **satisfice** rather than optimize - they simplify complex problems and choose an option that is satisfactory and sufficient rather than the best possible option.

### Intuition
- **Intuitive decision-making** is a non-conscious process developed through concentrated experience.
- It is fast, uses holistic associations between pieces of information, and is emotionally charged.
- Intuition is not necessarily wrong and can complement rational analysis, but intuitive hypotheses should still be tested using objective information and analysis.

## Common Decision-Making Biases
- **Overconfidence bias** is excessive confidence in one's own or others' abilities and judgments.
  - People with weaker intellectual or interpersonal abilities may be especially likely to overestimate their performance.
- **Anchoring bias** is placing too much weight on initial information and failing to adjust sufficiently when later information appears.
- **Confirmation bias** is seeking and overweighting information that supports existing beliefs while discounting contradictory information.
- **Availability bias** is basing judgments on information that is easiest to recall.
  - Recent, vivid, emotional, or personally experienced events can therefore be overestimated.
- **Escalation of commitment** is continuing with a decision despite clear evidence that it is wrong, especially when the decision-maker feels personally responsible for the outcome.
- **Loss aversion** is experiencing the pain of a loss more strongly than the pleasure of an equivalent gain.
- **Hindsight bias** is falsely believing, after an outcome is known, that it would have been accurately predicted beforehand.
