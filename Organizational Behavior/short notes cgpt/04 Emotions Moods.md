# Organizational Behaviour - Lesson 4: Emotions and Moods

## Emotion, Feeling, Mood, and Affect
- **Emotion** is an intense feeling directed at someone or something.
  - From the biological view in the slides, emotions involve chemicals released after we interpret a specific trigger and create a feedback loop between brain and body.
  - The slides state that the brain takes about one-quarter second to identify a trigger and another one-quarter second to produce the chemicals, which last for about six seconds.
- **Feeling** is what we sense as we integrate and think about an emotion.
  - Feelings are more cognitively processed, can combine several emotions, and usually last longer than the original emotion.
- **Mood** is a state of mind that is less intense than an emotion and does not necessarily require a specific contextual trigger.
  - Moods are influenced by environment, physiology, and mental state and can last from hours to days.
- **Affect** is the broad term covering feelings people experience, including emotions and moods.
- The lecture presents the sequence as **emotion first, then feeling, then mood** as emotional experiences are processed over time.
- Emotions are generally linked to a **specific event, brief duration, distinct expressions, and action**, while moods have more general causes, last longer, and are more cognitive.

## Positive and Negative Affect
- **Positive emotions** such as joy and gratitude express favourable evaluations; **negative emotions** such as anger and guilt express unfavourable evaluations.
- These categories also describe overall mood states called **positive affect and negative affect**.
- **High positive affect** includes excitement, enthusiasm, and elation; low positive affect includes boredom, depression, and fatigue.
- **High negative affect** includes nervousness, stress, and anxiety; low negative affect includes contentedness, calmness, and serenity.

## Functions of Emotions and Moods
- Emotions can support effective workplace functioning, especially through their relationship with **rationality and ethical judgment**.
- **Rationality and emotions are not necessarily opposites**.
  - Research discussed in the slides suggests that emotions provide context for understanding the world and are important to rational thinking.
  - Managers should recognize how emotions and moods affect them rather than automatically dismissing emotional responses as irrational.
- **Moral emotions** include sympathy, guilt, anger at injustice, contempt for unethical behaviour, and disgust at violations of moral norms.
  - Moral judgments are often strongly influenced by feelings, although fairness judgments can also contain a cognitive component.

## Sources of Emotions and Moods
- Influences come from **who a person is** and from **the person's circumstances at the time**.
- Personal influences include:
  - **Personality** - people have built-in tendencies to experience some moods and emotions more often than others.
  - **Affect intensity** - people differ in how strongly they experience positive and negative emotions.
  - **Age** - the study cited in the slides found fewer negative emotions with age, longer positive moods, and faster fading of bad moods among older people.
  - **Gender** - the slides report greater emotional expression and intensity among women, except for anger.
- Situational influences include:
  - **Time of day** - positive affect tends to be lowest early in the morning and highest in the evening; negative affect also varies across the day.
  - **Day of the week** - the slides report higher positive affect on Friday through Sunday and lower positive affect on Monday.
  - **Weather** - research presented in the slides suggests little effect on mood for most people.
  - **Stress** - stressful work events worsen moods, and the effects can accumulate over time.
  - **Social activity** - physical, informal, and shared eating activities are more strongly linked to positive mood than formal or sedentary activities.
  - **Sleep** - poor sleep increases fatigue and is linked to poorer mood, lower job satisfaction, and health risks.
  - **Exercise** - generally improves positive mood, with stronger effects for people who are depressed.
  - **Blood sugar** - low blood sugar is linked to irritability and disagreeableness, while excessively high blood sugar is linked in the slides to depression.

## Emotional Labour
- **Emotional labour** is the expression of organizationally desired emotions during interpersonal transactions at work and can be important to job performance.
- **Felt emotions** are a person's actual emotions; **displayed emotions** are the emotions the organization expects the employee to show.
- Display rules are learned, so displayed emotions may differ from what a person actually feels.
- **Surface acting** means hiding inner feelings and changing outward expression to meet display rules.
- **Deep acting** means trying to change the actual inner feeling so it matches the required display.
- **Emotional dissonance** occurs when an employee must display one emotion while actually feeling another.

## Affective Events Theory
- **Affective Events Theory (AET)** explains how workplace events create emotional reactions that influence job satisfaction and job performance.
- The lecture model links **work environment -> work events -> emotional reactions -> satisfaction and performance**.
  - Work events include daily hassles and daily uplifts.
  - Personality and current mood influence how strongly a person reacts to an event.
- People lower in emotional stability are more likely to react strongly to negative events.
- Emotional reactions can influence **organizational citizenship behaviour, organizational commitment, effort, intention to quit, and workplace deviance**.

## Emotional Intelligence
- Howard Gardner's theory of **multiple intelligences** challenged the idea that a single intelligence test captures all human ability and included linguistic, logical-mathematical, musical, spatial, body-kinesthetic, intrapersonal, interpersonal, and naturalist intelligence.
- Peter Salovey and John Mayer introduced the term **emotional intelligence (EI)** in 1990 and treated it as an ability, skill, or competency rather than a personality trait.
- In organizational behaviour, emotional intelligence is the ability to **recognize, understand, and manage one's own emotions and the emotions of others**.
- Emotional intelligence involves the ability to:
  - **Perceive emotions** in oneself and others.
  - **Understand the meaning of emotions**.
  - **Regulate emotions**.
- The lecture's emotional intelligence model also emphasizes **self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, and relationship management**.
- The **Cascading Model of Emotional Intelligence** links conscientiousness with perceiving emotions, cognitive ability with understanding emotions, and emotional stability with regulating emotions.
- High emotional intelligence can support **teamwork, communication, leadership, conflict resolution, employee satisfaction, organizational culture, and productivity**.

## Developing Emotional Regulation and Intelligence
- Personal emotion-regulation strategies in the lecture include **mindfulness, cognitive reappraisal, journaling, breathing techniques, physical activity, social support, boundaries, self-compassion, problem solving, and professional help when needed**.
- Organizations can strengthen emotional intelligence through **training, coaching and mentoring, feedback, open communication, mindfulness practices, team building, leadership development, diversity and inclusion, conflict-resolution training, and regular assessment**.
- Emotionally and socially intelligent people are better able to **understand and express themselves, relate to others, manage emotions, remain motivated, and cope with daily demands**.
